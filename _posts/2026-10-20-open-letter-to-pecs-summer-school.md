Dear Summer School,

I am a student at the University of Technology, Sydney, that was lucky enough to get to travel to Hungary to attend the School in July this year.

I really enjoyed my time in Pecs and it's hard to believe I was only there for a few weeks when that experience felt so formative and memorable. I have many thoughts about the school and I regret not having written them down in depth in the feedback form. I hope this written testimonial will partially make up for that and that my feedback will benefit the school, or at the very least provide encouragement and thanks to the team.

Here it is in the form of an open letter to you that I will also be publishing in my blog.

## Structure

I really liked the structure of the program. As in, every day starts with a 3 hour lecture, followed by lunch, with some social activity occurring in the evening. I think the reason the program as a whole was so effective at immersing its students was because there was only enough structure to keep everyone together and sticking to a sane sleep schedule, but otherwise allowing us complete autonomy and freedom.

Maybe this perspective makes more sense when you consider that in Sydney (and Australia in general), due to the population being concentrated in the cities with no-one in between, students can get away living at home rather than moving to student accommodation or housing near a campus. The super high cost of living also incentivizes working and disincentivizes moving out if your parents will let you stay at home when you turn 18. The effect of this is that hardly any undergraduate students live on campus (most commute between 40 and 100 minutes one way 2-4 times a week), and in Sydney especially since campuses are small or non existent in the city, correspondingly so is student life. You can find a spot near the main uni buildings, and go into debt renting it, but you'll be one of the only students doing so. So you can imagine what it was like for me to come from that world, where the average student has a 30 hour work week with online lectures in evenings, to a student town (which also does not exist in Australia) where I'm surrounded by students who have their schedules completely wiped, with literally nothing to do other than the arranged program.

The idea to have lectures be "mandatory" with a single forgoable lecture (and marked attendance) was very smart. This was the single reason we had butts in seats, full houses every morning which contributed heavily to student engagement in classes. If this condition were ever removed, attendance would plummet and the backbone of the school would collapse. But, importantly, despite the attendance requirement being in the official correspondence, it was not taken so seriously. Professors were very flexible with attendance and (I know you can't acknowledge this but) they did not penalize the few students who missed many lectures, producing the effect of high attendance rates without the consequence for those who really could not attend. Our single, ungraded assignment, which was to prepare a presentation about our country for the purposes of testing our English presentation skills (pretty pointless for me but hugely fun) was again only enough work to create something to present, nothing more to distract me from having fun. *This* was one of the biggest enablers of the adventures I went on and the reason I'm still thinking back to the School months later.

As a Sydneysider, I was fascinated by the concept of a uni town. This is a town with a campus spread across it that students live and study in and around. Every day I had about 5-6 hours of free time which I spent with my friends visiting pubs, going on walks, playing volleyball, chess, and cards, and exploring the city. There was enough time to party, recover, party again and still sleep 8 hours, which was a welcome change of pace from the daily grind back home.

## Classroom Content

I took the subject European Studies. As an Engineering student with an interest in the humanities, I found the academic part of the program much more interesting than I bet many of my humanities-major classmates did. The lectures were well chosen to complement each other and provide a gentle progression as well as constituting a well-rounded course on European Studies. I also think the structure and layout was thought out and effective at giving me, a student without a background in foreign policy or political science, more than an introduction to European Studies. Not only was it a good taster, which anyone can say about any introductory lecture ever, I really believe I now have a concrete understanding of the lecture contents (History of EU, Sustainable Development in Europe, Economic and Social challenges in Europe, etc.) I doubt the coordinators expected anyone to walk away from the school with as much as I did. Maybe we can chalk that up to a genuine interest in the subject, willingness to learn, learning environment of the program, and the lecturers themselves.

The lecturers! Oh my gosh, having only taken classes in humanities in high school by teachers who have been educated in English speaking countries, I wasn't sure what to expect in terms of quality. Of course I was trying not to assume but in Engineering at least, the communications portion of Engineering is not assessed sufficiently in postgraduate education. The result of this is that many Engineering professors at UTS need not be fluent English speakers and they sometimes do a substandard job at communicating the course content and successfully engaging students when we are discussing subjects as technically dense as data structures and algorithms.

The university professors at Pecs all spoke excellent English, moreover the quality of lecturing- which included organization of content, professionalism of presentation, eloquence and fluency of rhetoric, willingness to debate and have their views challenged, and success in engaging hungover and sleep deprived students- was absolutely outstanding. It was very refreshing to see professors teaching the way they are supposed to, and I am slightly ashamed to say in my 3 years of taking classes at my home university I am still yet to encounter such teaching here. Of all teachers, Professors Voros and Tarrosy stood out to me as exceptional in terms of subject matter expertise and teaching quality.

Sometimes we hear stories about lectures or classes that are so profound and inspiring that they convince students to enroll in degrees or write their theses on a certain topic. Professor Tarrosy's lecture on Political Cultures covered topics as broad as:

- Migration
- Colonialism in the world and propaganda and racism in Hungary today
- The communist history of Hungary (and his experiences as a person within it)
- Hungary during the Orban government and how it affected academia
- Shifting political identity amongst Hungarian youth

This was that lecture for me.

## Non Academic Activities

I really enjoyed the activities, and how fun they turned out to be really surprised me. I have to give my classmates kudos for participating with such enthusiasm. I remember before the school had even started, sitting in the front row during the opening ceremony. I was already impressed by the quality of speeches given by the opening speakers, and midway through when I heard the faint sound of music coming from the doorway and turned my head to see the brass band march into the hall my jaw visibly dropped. The performance was amazing and that absolutely set the tone for the entire rest of the school.

My favorite activity was dragon boat racing by the lake. We all forgot which team we were in by the second race, but I still think giving us teams to start with was a good idea to get us to socialize a bit and force us in the water. I also really liked the dance night- it was crazy to me that the Summer School was actually endorsing pregaming before showing up 🤯 that is something you will absolutely never hear at an Australian uni. I screenshotted that directive and sent it to all my friends back home.

But really, there wasn't a single activity I participated in and didn't enjoy. The folk dance class, the walk to the city and through the mosque and church, the lights festival, multiple dinners, contemporary dance workshop, language class, DOTTO train through city, I adored all of it and I loved the variety- some physical activities, some cultural, some purely fun. Everyone's willingness to be there (even though everything was optional), and be friendly, and talk to me even if they had already made friends was really cool. I think that the ethos of the program centers on being open to new things, and once people are thinking that way, connecting with them comes naturally.

## Organization and Logistics

This is the part where I praise the deliberate choices and direct efforts of the Summer School team. The program (from my perspective) ran so smoothly and without any problems at all, we stuck to schedule to the minute, everything took exactly as long as it was supposed to. I think one afternoon activity was moved from Tuesday to Thursday because of weather or something tiny like that, but really there were no surprises. The schedule was sent to us via groupchat daily and everything was done to make following the plan as straightforward as possible.

The facilities were exceptional, I thought the dorm I was given was very generous- it was clean and spacious (two of us in a three person dorm, and far more cupboards and drawers than I used). The bathroom was clean and spacious. Even the kitchen was clean and spacious! For 4 people sharing a kitchen, we never ran out of space, and had 2-3 other people with us most days. On top of that, I am not sure how intentional this was (probably not at all) but at in the European Studies school amongst the male students, we were all paired up very fittingly according to personality. All of us got along very well with our dormmates (Better than I would have with any other student) and my roommate in particular ended up becoming a good friend who I have caught up with multiple times since the school ended.

The laundry machines were free, the dorm provided drying racks and ironing boards and many other resources we did not need for free. There was a grocery a minute walk away and a chemist next to it. All of this might be necessary for full time students, but for students that are only here temporarily for less than a month it was extremely professional and well resourced. I would highly recommend this accommodation to future students. The temporary student ID, free transport reusable bus ticket, meal ticket system, enrollment in NEPTUN, and other prearranged/prepaid services were also extremely professional and I definitely took advantage of them. It was fantastic that absolutely everything, essentially 100% of the program was arranged logistically, and all I had to do (if I wanted to do the bare minimum) was follow the mentors around.

The bistro food was really, really good. The portion sizes were hearty and food was sumptuous and filling (and surprisingly diverse). I also appreciated that every single meal that was arranged had really nice dietary accommodations, such as the vegetarian alternatives. Sometimes I took advantage of these and sometimes I didn't. I genuinely was not prepared for the food at the school to be so good, especially the chances to try higher-end restaurants, like the provided dinners. I (and other students) would not be able to afford such nice experiences back home.

The weather was much warmer than I expected. It was really uncomfortable actually and woke me up at 5am every morning, but all part of the experience I guess. It would feel like cheating to get to live in Pecs but somehow avoid the pecsi weather.

The cost of living in Pecs is very low, bare necessities are roughly half the price of what they would be in Sydney, and certain foods like bread and liquor are 1/5-1/3 of the price. I paid the full enrollment price of 1000 euros for the program fees which I would be more than willing to pay again. I realize the program is likely being heavily subsidized and this is not the true cost per student, but if I had to put a dollar value on how much the program was worth to me it would be many multiples of that. I also realize that part of the appeal of the school is its comparable affordability, so this figure may be (reasonably) fixed for now.

## Student Mentors

The student mentors ABSOLUTELY held the entire program together, I am not sure why I left it so late in the letter to mention them.

It is super clear to me that if the (secondary) purpose of the school is to form friendships then this can be made so much easier with sufficient social lubricant. The mentors did a great job with the easy stuff like giving us meal tickets, walking us to class, being a positive smiling face, and generic help regarding where we could find X or Y.

But the real value came from the group chat with our class in it and the mentors taking turns to give us time every day to lead an activity for us. Often something as simple as going to a bar or for a walk and opening the invite to the group. This made it SO much easier to connect with the others in my class and get out of the dorm, because the mental load of having to find something to do and people to do it with was eliminated, and the mentors were so personable and friendly they were more than capable of bringing the fun on their own (not that we would let them).

If we didn't have the mentors, I don't think I would have spoken to half the people I did or do half the things that I was pushed to do. The program would look very different, much more academic and less fun. The mentors make me think of someone that studies at Pecs as relaxed, confident, and enjoying their student life.

My mentors went as far as spending their own time with my friends and I, inviting us out and accepting our invites to hang out. It didn't feel like they were stingy with time at all. Or maybe we just struck gold with our three (Vicki, Tina, and Ada, if you are reading this I hope you realize you made the experience for us!) This program would not have been nearly as successful without them, my best memories were out of schedule side quests with the mentors.

## Constructive Criticism

- I did not really enjoy the lecture on Cultural Sensitivity by Dr Pozsgai. I didn't understand it and it didn't seem to fit into European Studies at all. It wasn't political or historical or cultural. I remember most of the content of this lecture as stuff that was taught to us in primary school. I would have liked this lecture to have been replaced by another more traditional politics/history class.
- The teacher who gave the dance workshop on Hungarian folk dance, I cannot remember her name, but she could benefit from giving a few more classes as she seemed inexperienced and unconfident. She was a wonderful teacher, I just think more practice might make the class more cohesive and engaging.
- I am conflicted about the library/museum class- on one hand, I think it was a critical cultural and historical lesson, but the summer heat and standing on my feet for so long (and 10 days of sleep deprivation) made it very hard to focus. I think more than half the class fell asleep during the museum portion of the lecture, when we sat down.
- I regret Professor Tarrosy not being able to DJ for us during the dance night.
- My least favorite activity was basketball. I think everyone was just really tired from the night before, so the turnout wasn't great. I also thought that the "Intercultural Evening" where we sat in circles and answered the prompts was a bit dry.

## Personal Impact

I can't understate the effect this program has had on me. It has altered the trajectory of my life and fully convinced me to pursue higher education- potentially in Pecs, but in a student town for sure. A large part of this is due to everything I have written here, the concrete tangible stuff, but equally it is due to the friendships and experiences with people that I had that I can't really capture in words or attribute to anything more than chance, and something special in the lovely Hungarian air.

Please consider this email proof of the immense success of the Summer School from the perspective of a student, and appreciation for the Summer School team and their efforts.
