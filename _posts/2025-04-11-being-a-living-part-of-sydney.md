## What is Sydney?

Any question phrased so succinctly and trivially cannot have a definitive, concise answer. The question demands to be explored in depth. It demands the answerer reference Australia's aboriginal heritage, colonial history, demographics and migration patterns, socio-cultural evolution, economic state, political partition, geographical boundaries, and countless other subjective ways of defining a city. In this post I want to talk about how Sydney is its population. Not just the emergent property of a collection of people, but more precisely how insignificant individuals can have a very *felt* presence in a city of millions. How you can control the size of your cultural contribution.

## People make a place

It is one thing to be a powerful politician, like our current prime minister Albanese or an influential public figure, like Danny Lim, or even people who did nothing to stand out but ended up going down in history nonetheless like Kathleen Folbigg. These people definitely have made a measurable contribution to Sydney's recent history. But the very fact that they are named somehow makes their contribution selfish. Not in a bad way. But as though the life they have lived and the things they have done are tied to their identities first and foremost and only through incidence are associated with Sydney itself. I think the most powerful contributions people make to the culture of a city is an *implicit one*. A contribution that can't be named, but one whose effects are observable. 

## How Can a Meaningful Contribution be Implicit?

The Macca's at Town Hall on George St is the most famous Macca's in all of Sydney, probably Australia. The first time I dined at this dignified establishment was when I had graduated school and found myself spending some late nights in the city. I grew up far away from fast food and I prefer home cooked food, so there's really not a lot on offer for me at any big fast food chain.

But being there, on the grimy seats under the bright lights, was different to any other restaurant in the city. the Town Hall Macca's is the perfect blend of these two things that are really important to me. Firstly security, fast food is the epitome of security. I am always conscious of what I am going to eat and if a place has options for me. If I see a chain I recognize, I know it will a) have standards (cleanliness, service, etc.), b) have dietary options, and c) be reasonably cheap. Although Macca's is not renowned for the healthiest foods, the fact that it is "safe" and familiar is enough for me to prefer eating fries there to risking it at a nice place next door.

But the Town Hall Macca's in particular also boasts another property that just makes it perfect: it is busy. I don't care if your restaurant staff are totally indifferent or have seen it all. If I'm at a nice place, I can't shake the feeling of being judged. I can always put up with it but it'll never be my preference. But THM is just so busy and there are so many people there that *no one cares about you*. There's just too many orders and too much to do, too many drunk people throwing up, too many crackheads having sex in the bathrooms.

The first time I went to the THM I didn't even order, and no-one knew or cared. My friends and I sat at a table for literal hours. On top of all of this, the fact that the THM is essentially 24/7 is all I need, I could not wish for a better, more convenient place to eat. The THM is what it means to "enjoy Sydney like a local".

This is my experience with THM. Everyone has their own unique experience and relationship with it, and you're not a real Sydneysider until you've been there. My mentor from work, for example, was telling me what he got up to over the weekend and the THM was a central location in his recount. It means something to him too.

It's the fact that the THM is so small and approachable that allows people to come together to create and maintain what is really an icon of Sydney to people that actually live here, more than the Opera House or Harbour Bridge.

It is by being employed at the THM, likely between the ages of 14 and 22, that an insignificant, nameless teen can interract with so many people from all suburbs of Sydney and contribute to and truly be a part of Sydney's living heritage. To be one of the small team that has the honour of serving the community through this cultural institution.

## The Central Tunnel

![Central tunnel](https://imgur.com/B9RJ1BB.png)

One other really fascinating example of "being a living part of Sydney" is busking in the tunnels. Yeah it is a bit shady and there are often people experiencing homelessness there- in fact, most buskers probably aren't busking, but just begging for money which is actually really sad. But that tunnel (which I just learnt is called the Devonshire Street Tunnel) is universally known by all Sydneysiders. We've all walked there, probably run through there at some point as a student, and seen someone we knew there going the other way. Especially UTS students. I know it has negative connotations and I really don't mean to idealise or romanticise homelessness. Singing to the public in that tunnel to me means the same as an overworked underpayed teen doing the night shift at the THM, they're contributing to the shared experience of a place we've all been to and had not just exciting, but mundane experiences in.

## More Examples

Open up instagram and find a popular Sydney based shitposter. There are plenty, and all they do is make jokes about these shared experiences that are often tiny and forgettable to tourists- yet places or events we all know about. The unspoken shared experience. Something I can talk about to anyone, assuming they will understand. And they always do.

## Footnote: My Defense to Anyone That Says "We Are All Equal Parts of Sydney"

The introductory quote might give some readers the wrong idea, that we are all here building Sydney together. The same way a school is literally its students or a company is its employees. I don't deny that we are what makes the city, but that's not really something I've intended to express in this post- rather that an enormous shared culture hinges on a surprisingly small and *knowable* foundation. 

It is hard for individuals to impress their own value and individuality when part of a collective, especially as that collective scales.

### Creating Community at Scale

Last year I attended the NCSS Summer Camp. How does a cohort of 100 kids develop a culture? Naturally some kids are more outgoing. Others by circumstance get picked on by the tutors (in a good way) or make a fool of themselves in front of others and an inside joke starts to develop. And I saw it firsthand, 5 - 10 kids started to become representative of that particular cohort of 100 students, because they were loud or fun or cool or smart or active on the Discord or just because. And most of them were invited back as returners. I saw this previously at WSC when I was 14 and again last month when I was in Adelaide for the ARC.

Maybe your class in high school had it too, the students who would organize the birthday card for the teacher. I might be overanalysing, but these were often students who felt like they were part of the class and felt obligated to do something "for the class". And just like that they manifest their perceived centrality. They become the kid that organizes.

By the time you get to the size of a local community- maybe 1000s of people, like the Ingleburn community, it is quite hard to have special individuals that represent a community, whose voices are "louder" than the rest naturally, without intentionally picking them. People who identify with and are more involved with the spirit of the group than others. But they show up, in the form of the guy who always goes way too big with Christmas decorations, or the librarian who has been working there longer than most of us have been alive. This is my defense to anyone who claims "We Are All Equal Parts of Sydney". Yeah maybe we all live here and maybe the empty can on the floor that I picked up and tossed in the bin, or the bubble tea I bought are doing their own little bit to influence the economy and culture of Sydney. But some people are just more important than others. In a way I go to uni and build the UTS environment for non uni students, but the scale of UTS (thousands attend UTS every day) makes me just that much less special than the small number with such considerable influence in other places.

While employees of the Opera House and Sydney Bridge Climb and security guards at the ICC probably get a lot of traffic too, the exclusivity of these venues takes away from how many people can understand them. I mean, how many people do you know that you can guarantee have been to Sydney Acquarium? But anyone can go to, and work at, the THM.

## In Conclusion

In conclusion I find it interesting that ordinary people build the shared experiences, and the culture, of the city we live in.
